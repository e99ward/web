A FIELD GUIDE TO FURNACE CONTROL

Three terms.
One temperature.

P responds to the error now. I remembers the error over time. D responds to how fast the temperature is changing.

Heater demand
P + I + D → 0–100%
The controller changes power. The furnace determines how fast temperature follows.
01 / THE CONTROL LOOP

Start with the temperature error.

The setpoint r(t) is your programmed heating profile. The measured furnace temperature is T(t). Their difference is the error:

e(t)=r(t)−T(t)

Positive error means the furnace is too cold. Negative error means it is hotter than the setpoint.

Heating profile r(t)
↓   compare with T(t)
Error e(t)
↓   PID controller
Heater demand u(t)
↓   furnace + sensor
Temperature T(t) ↺ feedback

The standard parallel PID equation

uraw(t)=Kpe(t)+Ki∫0te(τ)dτ+Kddedt
The original gain mode and the examples below use D on temperature. That option uses −Kd dT/dt instead of Kd de/dt, with a derivative filter. The new time mode defaults to derivative on error. These match during a constant setpoint hold, but differ during ramps because de/dt = dr/dt − dT/dt. Derivative on measurement avoids a power spike when the setpoint jumps.
u=clamp0,100[Kpe+Ki∫edt−KddTdt]Conceptual equation; the implementation filters D and conditionally accumulates I.
NEW / FILTERED PID WITH TIME PARAMETERS

Tv sets the strength. Td sets the smoothing.

Gc(s)=Kp[1+1Tns+Tvs1+Tds]U(s) = Gc(s)E(s), for zero initial conditions, derivative on error, and operation before output limiting.

The workbench now offers this time-parameter form. Tn is the integral/reset time, Tv is the derivative action time, and Td is the derivative filter time. All three are measured in minutes.

The notation changed: in the earlier ideal equation, Td named the derivative action time. In this new equation, that role belongs to Tv; Td now names the denominator's filter time. Its equivalent parallel name is Tf.

Equivalent parallel gains

Ki=KpTn,Kd=KpTv

Smaller Tn strengthens I. Larger Tv strengthens D. Changing Kp with these times fixed scales P, I and D together. Tv = 0 turns D off; the Integral enabled switch turns I off without using an invalid zero Tn.

The derivative has its own dynamics

TdduDdt+uD=KpTvdedt

A larger Td smooths and slows the derivative response. For Td > 0, its high-frequency gain approaches KpTv/Td, rather than growing without bound. Td = 0 recovers an unfiltered derivative.

For Kp = 2.4, Tn = 20 min, Tv = 1.6667 min and Td = 0.25 min, the equivalent gains are Ki = 0.12 and Kd ≈ 4. The derivative input can be error or measured temperature; choose Error to match the displayed transfer function. The gain experiments below retain derivative on temperature for continuity.

This corresponds to the filtered parallel form Kp + Ki/s + Kds/(1 + Tfs), with Tf = Td. Source: MathWorks filtered PID documentation ↗

02 / WHAT EACH GAIN DOES

Present error. Past error. Temperature trend.

P

ProportionalRespond to the gap

uP=Kpe

A larger error immediately asks for more power. Raising Kp usually makes tracking faster and reduces the temperature gap.

Too much P: with heater lag and delay, the controller can react too strongly and produce overshoot or oscillation. Once demand is already 100%, increasing P cannot add more power.

EXAMPLE

e = 20 °C, Kp = 2 %/°C
uP = 40%

Kp units: % / °C
I

IntegralRemember the gap

uI=Ki∫edt

A small error that persists keeps increasing the integral contribution. At a reachable hold temperature, I can supply the power needed to balance heat loss even when the error becomes zero.

Too much I: accumulated demand can keep heating after the temperature catches up. This can create overshoot and slow recovery. Anti-windup limits accumulation into output saturation.

EXAMPLE

e = 10 °C for 5 min, Ki = 0.2
ΔuI = 0.2 × 10 × 5 = 10%

Ki units: % / (°C · min)
D

DerivativeBrake the trend

uD=−KddTdt

When temperature rises rapidly, D subtracts heater demand. When it falls, D adds demand. It can damp oscillation and help the controller approach a hold more smoothly.

Too much D: heating can become sluggish, and sensor noise can strongly affect power. D cannot remove heat or erase steady-state error by itself. Its benefit depends on the other gains and the profile.

EXAMPLE · UNFILTERED

dT/dt = 5 °C/min, Kd = 4
uD = −20%

Kd units: % · min / °C
Together, these example terms give 40 + 10 − 20 = 30% heater demand. The contributions add before the output is limited to 0–100%. A negative total turns the heater off; this model has passive cooling.

04 / WHY THE FURNACE MATTERS

Power is immediate. Heat is delayed.

Even after demand drops, stored heater energy can keep warming the furnace. Our model separates commanded power u from effective heating h:

τhdhdt+h=u(t−θ)

τh is thermal lag; θ is transport delay. Both make aggressive gains more likely to overshoot.

Temperature rises with effective heater power and falls with heat loss to ambient:

dTdt=Hh100−λ(T−Ta)

H is maximum heating in °C/min; λ is heat loss in 1/min; Ta is ambient temperature. Here h and u are percentages.

Why P alone leaves a gap during a hold

At steady temperature, required heater demand is uss = 100λ(Tss − Ta)/H. With P only, uss = Kp(r − Tss). Combining them gives:

ess=λ(r−Ta)λ+HKp/100For an unsaturated P-only controller at a constant setpoint, with H > 0 and λ > 0.

For r = 600 °C, Ta = 25 °C, H = 18 °C/min, λ = 0.012/min and Kp = 2.4 %/°C, the hold error is approximately 15.5 °C. Integral action can close this gap if the closed loop is stable and the target is reachable.

A gain cannot overcome insufficient heater capacity. With positive heat loss, the full-power equilibrium is Tmax = Ta + H/λ. At the default furnace settings it is 1,525 °C. Targets at or above this limit cannot be reached and held in finite time by increasing PID gains.
05 / READING A HEATING PROFILE

Ramp, approach, hold, cool.

PhaseWhat you seeHow the terms contribute
RampThe setpoint rises at the programmed rate.P responds to tracking error; I accumulates persistent lag; measurement-based D subtracts demand while temperature rises. A fast ramp may exceed available heating power.
Approach to holdThe setpoint stops rising, but the furnace may keep warming.P declines as the gap closes. I retains its history. D can damp the rising temperature; stored heat and delay can still cause overshoot.
HoldThe setpoint stays constant.P corrects disturbances. I supplies the ongoing heat-loss demand with near-zero error. D damps rapid temperature changes and approaches zero at steady temperature.
Cool downThe furnace loses heat to ambient.This simulator forces demand to zero during final cooling. PID cannot speed that cooling; residual heater energy may briefly keep temperature rising.

A useful learning sequence

  1. Start with P only. Set I and D to zero. Increase P and compare faster tracking with oscillation and hold error.
  2. Add I gradually. Observe how the hold error shrinks and how the transition into a hold changes.
  3. Add D if damping helps. Compare the entire ramp and hold, rather than assuming a larger D always reduces overshoot.
  4. Repeat for your profile. A setting that works for one ramp rate, load, or delay may respond differently in another.
Controller conventions vary. Some instruments use integral time Ti and derivative time Td in u = Kp[e + (1/Ti)∫e dt + Td de/dt]. For that ideal form, Ki = Kp/Ti and Kd = KpTd. A smaller Ti means stronger integral action. Proportional band and vendor-specific settings need their own conversion.
